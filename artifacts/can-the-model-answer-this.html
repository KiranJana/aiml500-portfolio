Title

Can the Model Answer This? A Good Fit / Danger Zone check for your first AI assistant

  • Artifact 3
  • Checklist
  • Large language models
  • Hallucination

Introduction

The first demo of an internal AI assistant usually goes well. It explains a concept clearly, drafts a decent message and sounds sure of itself. The trouble starts when someone asks it about a service that changed last week, or how many incidents the team had last quarter, and it answers just as smoothly with something that isn't true. This page is a short check for deciding which questions an assistant can answer from the model alone and which ones need a tool, a document or a person behind them. It comes from a course exercise where I played the part of a language model and caught myself making up an answer.

Description

In the exercise, an AI tutor called Professor Predict asked me to finish sentences with whatever felt most likely, without looking anything up. "The capital of France is" was easy. Then it asked me to finish this one:

"The current president of the small island nation of Velmora, founded in 1998, is ___"

I suspected Velmora wasn't a real country, and I still wrote "John Newgate," because the sentence was asking for a name. Velmora was made up. That small moment is the mechanism behind a lot of AI mistakes. A language model is pretrained to predict the next word from the words before it, so a sentence that ends in "is ___" pulls toward something shaped like an answer, whether or not a real answer exists. Researchers at OpenAI and Georgia Tech showed that this pretraining creates pressure toward made-up answers even with error-free training data: facts that appear only once or rarely in the data lead to errors, while patterns that repeat, such as grammar, don't (Kalai et al., 2026).

Assistant models then get more training to follow instructions. OpenAI's InstructGPT work showed that training with human feedback made answers more truthful, but the models still made simple mistakes (Ouyang et al., 2022). Nothing in either stage checks each answer against reality. So the useful question for a team is which kinds of questions are risky, and what to put behind them.

The ten tasks I classified

The last part of the exercise gave me ten tasks to sort into Good Fit or Danger Zone, assuming the model had no search, calculator, database or supplied document. These are my calls and my reasons, with the backup each Danger Zone task needs.

Objective

I did the original exercise for Assignment 3.3 in AIML-500, an interactive session with an AI tutor about how large language models are trained and what that means for what they can and can't do reliably. It had four stages: finishing sentences as if I were the model, explaining the mechanism in my own words, guessing what kinds of text end up in training data, and sorting tasks into Good Fit or Danger Zone. For this portfolio, I turned my answers into a check that engineers and team leads can use while they decide what their first internal assistant should answer on its own.

Process

  1. Played the model. I finished three sentences without looking anything up: Paris, "once upon a time," and the made-up president of Velmora. The third one is the lesson. I had a feeling the country wasn't real and filled the blank anyway.
  2. Explained it in my own words. My explanation was that at the end of the day the model has to generate an output. The tutor added two things I hadn't said: confidence is a pattern too, so a model sounds sure because confident text is common, and nobody has to be trying to trick it, because ordinary questions about obscure real facts cause the same problem.
  3. Guessed the training mix, and got corrected. I rated Wikipedia "a little" and full academic papers "a lot," because I was thinking about which sources are trustworthy. The question was how much of each a model sees. Wikipedia and public code are everywhere in web data, while paywalled papers are much rarer. I had also described training text as labeled right or wrong. It isn't: pretraining just predicts the next word, so a wrong sentence gets learned the same way as a right one.
  4. Sorted ten tasks. I classified the tasks in the table above and gave a reason for each one. The biggest surprise for me was low-resource languages. Models seem multilingual, but the training data is uneven, so output in those languages can sound fluent and still be wrong, and I wouldn't be able to tell.
  5. Checked the lessons against research. I didn't want this page to rest on what a chatbot told me, so I looked for a primary source behind each Danger Zone and read its abstract. Next-word pretraining and rare facts: Kalai et al. (2026). Exact arithmetic: when transformer models were tested on multi-digit multiplication, they matched patterns they had seen instead of learning a reliable method, and the authors argue that accuracy can fall off quickly as problems get more complex (Dziri et al., 2023). Low-resource languages: good translation needs large amounts of parallel text, which isn't equally available across the world's 7,000+ languages (NLLB Team et al., 2024). Supplying documents: models that retrieve passages before answering produced more specific and factual text (Lewis et al., 2020). Tools: language models struggle with arithmetic and fact lookup, and learning to call a calculator or search engine helped (Schick et al., 2023).
  6. Turned it into a check. I grouped the Danger Zone reasons into five questions and drew the flowchart, so a team can run a new kind of question through it in under a minute.

What I'd tell a team building its first internal assistant

  • Questions about your own systems are the "private" case. The model has never seen your runbooks, service names or incident history. Give it the documents with the question, and show which document the answer came from so people can check it.
  • "Is the payments service healthy right now?" is the "live" case. Have the assistant call the monitoring API and report what it gets back. Don't let it guess from what it learned in training.
  • Counts, totals and latency percentiles are the "exact" case. Let the assistant write and run the query, then report the number from the result.
  • Put a Velmora question in your test set. Ask the assistant about a service or a runbook that doesn't exist, and check that it says it can't find it instead of describing one.
  • Make "I don't know" an acceptable answer, and don't judge the assistant only by how many questions it answers. Kalai et al. (2026) found that scoring by accuracy alone rewards guessing over admitting uncertainty.

Where this check falls short

  • Five "no" answers don't make an answer correct. A Good Fit task can still go wrong, which is why the chart says to read the answer before using it.
  • The five questions are my own grouping from one course exercise and a handful of papers. I haven't tested the check on a real assistant yet, so it is a starting point for a team's own rules, not a standard.
  • For the research, I read each paper's abstract and checked my sentences against it. I didn't work through the full experiments.
  • Many assistants now have search or code tools built in. Those change which backup is already there, but someone still has to check that the tool was actually used.

Value Proposition

This artifact shows that I understand why language models make things up, and that I can turn that understanding into a practical rule a team can apply. For a team building its first internal assistant, it gives a quick way to decide where the model can answer on its own and where it needs search, documents, a calculator or a person, before users start trusting its answers.

Unique Value

Most advice about AI mistakes stops at "always double-check the output." This check says which questions need checking and what kind of backup each one needs. It starts from a mistake I made myself, and every Danger Zone on it is tied to a published paper. It also translates the course examples into the questions an engineering team actually asks: service status, internal runbooks and incident counts.

Relevance

Most teams' first AI project is an assistant that answers questions about their own work, and those are the questions a model is least able to answer from training alone, because they are private, current or exact. An assistant that answers them confidently and wrongly loses people's trust quickly. Knowing where the Danger Zones are is what tells a team which tools, documents and reviews to build in from the start.
